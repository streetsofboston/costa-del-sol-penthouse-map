Gardening the terrace & solarium

Ideas for turning Unit B's two outdoor floors into green, semi-private outdoor rooms — small trees, planting and plant-grown shade — without ever closing off the sea. The view is mostly south, opening to the south-east, so those edges stay low and open; all the height, screening and shade-making goes to the west end and the north side.

The ground rules

Six principles every scheme below follows. The starting point is what's already drawn on the plan: the covered terrace at the west end of the main floor, and the pergola formed by Bedroom 4's roof on the solarium. The planting builds around those two shade anchors rather than adding new structures.

01

South & south-east stay low

A slim planter line along the south edge and around the south-east wrap — lavender, trailing rosemary, ornamental grasses. A green frame at knee height that you look over, so the view stays wide at both levels.

02

Height at the west end & north side

Potted olive and lemon trees and troughs of clumping bamboo go where they're needed: overlooking comes from the sides and from the block to the north, never from the sea. The west-end screen also shelters the terrace from the Poniente wind.

03

Zone with planters, not walls

Big planters make the "rooms": a green buffer around Bedroom 4's door so it sits in a garden, a planted edge around the pool, movable pots that can be rearranged as habits settle.

04

Shade grown, not built

Bougainvillea and grapevine over the pergola give dappled summer shade and let winter sun through once the vine is bare. Jasmine on a screen scents the evenings. Any shade sail must come down easily — the Levante wind will test it.

05

Plants that want to be here

Species proven in Estepona's sun, salt air and wind: olive, citrus, the native fan palm, bougainvillea, jasmine, lavender, rosemary, strelitzia. Forgiving plants, not prima donnas.

06

Water on a timer, weight over structure

Drip irrigation runs off the solarium's pre-installed shower water point, so August doesn't depend on anyone being home. The heaviest planters sit over walls and columns below, never mid-slab.

Three schemes to choose between

Same ground rules, three levels of commitment. They can also be mixed floor by floor — C · Lush on the solarium and A · Light touch on the main terrace is a perfectly sensible combination.

Scheme A · Light touch

Frame the view

The minimum that makes both floors feel gardened rather than bare.

  • One continuous low planter line along the south edge and south-east wrap of both floors
  • Two anchor trees only: an olive at the solarium's north-west corner, a lemon by the covered terrace
  • Jasmine trained on the covered terrace's open side; herbs by the kitchen door
  • Everything else stays open deck — maximum flexibility while you learn the sun and wind

Upkeep: about an hour a week in season. Timer irrigation does the rest. Easiest to change later — nothing is committed.

Scheme B · Balanced

The Mediterranean middle

Proper outdoor rooms and real privacy, with the view untouched. This is the sketches' default setting.

  • Full screens at the west end and along the north side: bamboo troughs plus olive and citrus in large pots
  • Planted pool edge and a green buffer around Bedroom 4's door
  • Grapevine and bougainvillea over the solarium pergola; dining under the covered terrace with jasmine
  • Paired citrus pots divide lounge from dining on the main terrace

Upkeep: a couple of hours a week in season, plus a spring and autumn tidy — pruning the vine, refreshing the lavender. The scheme most people would call "finished".

Scheme C · Lush

The rooftop garden

The solarium becomes a garden that happens to have a pool and a guest room in it.

  • Everything in Balanced, plus a small grove toward the north-east, fan palms at the corners, layered underplanting
  • Climbers on every screen until the west and north edges read as green walls
  • A shaded reading nook under the pergola and a planted path feel around Bedroom 4
  • Most privacy and most shade — and the most green between you and the neighbours

Upkeep: half a day a week in summer, or a gardener's visit every week or two. More water, more pruning, more leaf-fall in the pool — the price of the jungle.

The plant palette

Chosen for Estepona: full sun, salt-laden air, the Levante and Poniente winds, and long dry summers once the irrigation is doing its job.

Olive

Olea europaea

The anchor tree. One or two in the biggest pots, at the west end and along the north side. Slow, sculptural, nearly indestructible in a container with drainage.

Anchor tree · screen

Lemon & citrus

Citrus × limon

Glossy leaves, scented blossom, fruit by the kitchen. Pairs of pots work as soft dividers between terrace zones; keep out of the strongest wind lane.

Divider · scent · fruit

Mediterranean fan palm

Chamaerops humilis

The native palm, built for this coast — the most wind-hardy choice there is. Clumping habit makes it a screen and a statement in one pot.

Screen · wind-hardy

Clumping bamboo

Bambusa / Fargesia, clumping types only

The fastest privacy there is: a trough along the north side is a green wall within one season. Clumping varieties only — never running types in containers near drains.

Fast screen · windbreak

Bougainvillea

Bougainvillea spectabilis

Trained over the pergola and up the west screen: months of colour and real shade. Thorny — keep it off paths and the pool edge.

Climber · shade · colour

Grapevine

Vitis vinifera

The classic pergola plant: leafy shade all summer, bare in winter so the low sun gets through, and grapes as a bonus. Needs one firm prune a year.

Pergola shade

Star jasmine

Trachelospermum jasminoides

Evergreen, tidy, and the evening scent is the whole point — plant it where you sit after dinner: the covered terrace and beside Bedroom 4's door.

Scent · evergreen screen

Lavender & rosemary

Lavandula · Salvia rosmarinus (trailing)

The south-edge line: low mounds of lavender with trailing rosemary spilling over the planter edge, continued around the south-east wrap. Bee-friendly, drought-happy, view-safe forever.

Low edge · view frame

Bird of paradise

Strelitzia reginae

Sculptural leaves for the pool edge — reads tropical without needing tropical water. Flowers orange in spring once established.

Pool edge · accent

The practical bits

Weight comes first

A big planter full of wet soil and an olive tree is seriously heavy. The largest pots and troughs belong over the walls and columns below — along edges and above the flat's structure — not mid-slab. This decides where the anchor trees can go, so it's the first question for the developer's technician, alongside the pool's loading on the north strip.

Water is already there

The solarium's pre-installed shower point is a ready-made water source: drip lines on a timer can run from it, zoned so the thirsty citrus get more than the lavender. On the main terrace, a tap point and the same timer setup keep August survivable when nobody's home.

Mind the drainage

Planters sit on feet or rails, never flat over the terrace drains, and saucers shouldn't let water pond against the waterproofing. A quick check after the first big rain is worth more than any drawing.

Wind is a design input

Estepona gets both: the Poniente from the west and the gusty Levante. The west-end screen is the Poniente shelter that makes the south side calm; heavy, low-centred pots for anything tall; bamboo and fan palm flex rather than snap; shade sails only if they can be dropped in minutes.

Planting around the pool

The pool sits on the north strip beside Baño 3, opposite Bedroom 4 from the pergola — so its planting can be settled now: strelitzia and grasses along its edge, the loungers at the strip's far end, and no leaf-shedding trees over the water (bougainvillea drops bracts, so it stays on the pergola, on the other side of the room).

Bedroom 4 stays flexible

Its use isn't decided yet — guest suite, pool retreat, studio. Planters on castors for its green buffer means the planting works for any of them and can be re-arranged in an afternoon when the room finds its purpose.

Worth asking while the build is still fluid

  • Where are the load-bearing lines on both terraces — where exactly may the heaviest planters and troughs sit?
  • Can a second water point / irrigation feed be confirmed on the main terrace, and the solarium shower point's pressure checked for drip lines?
  • Does the community (comunidad) have rules on visible screening height or pergola planting that the schemes should respect?
  • Can the pool's edge detail on the north strip leave room for the planted border shown in the sketch?
